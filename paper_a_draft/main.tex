\begin{abstract}
Static score fusion can exploit complementarity between frozen multimodal knowledge graph completion models. Query-dependent adjustment is an option for responding to query heterogeneity, but can degrade an effective static reference. Anchored Dynamic Correction (ADC) maps a learned preference to a bounded adjustment around a development-selected Global weight, with confidence-based fallback. Its action bound gives no reciprocal-rank risk guarantee. On MKG-W and DB15K, with three paired base seeds, we evaluate four primary M-Hyper pairs and two additional architecture pairs. ADC gains 0.02--0.24 MRR percentage points over Global in three primary pairs, with conditional bootstrap intervals above zero. The remaining primary effect is slightly negative; its interval and both additional-pair intervals span zero. Healthy DynaSemble has higher MRR than ADC in two primary pairs, and Relation also exceeds ADC on MKG-W with M-Hyper + NativE (W-N). ADC has lower unconditional mean reciprocal-rank loss relative to Global than healthy DynaSemble in all four primary pairs; Global itself has zero reference-relative loss. Matched hard-clipped correction also has slightly higher MRR in all four primary pairs. A 14-parameter selector still requires both scorers: historical pre-repair total time was 6.2--11.8 times primary-only, and corrected timing is unavailable. Both datasets had been inspected during development, so this is a retrospective evaluation of general score post-processing in MMKGC. The evidence describes utility--loss and deployment trade-offs, without establishing independent confirmation, uniform superiority or end-to-end efficiency.
\end{abstract}
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} multimodal knowledge graph completion; model combination; dynamic score correction; negative transfer; selective adaptation.

\section{Introduction}

Multimodal knowledge graph completion (MMKGC) predicts missing facts using graph structure and entity-associated modalities. We study the setting in which two independently trained scoring models are already available for the same queries and candidate entities, for example when different architectures have been retained from earlier model development. Freezing those models lets us test score-level post-processing without changing their representations or repeating base-model training. The question is whether their existing scores can be combined usefully; running both scorers still incurs their inference cost.

M-Hyper, NativE and AdaMF-MAT process modality information through different architectures~\citep{mhyper,native,adamf}. Together with uneven image/text coverage in our inputs (Table~\ref{tab:data-modalities}), this motivates studying their heterogeneous rankings. These observations do not establish the cause of their accuracy differences or make complementarity uniquely multimodal. Our post-processing uses candidate-score distributions and direction, while known-entity coverage and relation diagnostics characterize the MMKGC application setting.

A globally weaker model can still contribute useful rankings. For example, on MKG-W a development-selected static mixture of M-Hyper and NativE outperforms either standalone scorer (Table~\ref{tab:claims-main}). This establishes useful static combination for that pair, not that query-dependent weights can be learned from the available features or improve on Global. We therefore measure the benefit and loss of any subsequent adjustment relative to Global.

Dynamic weighting can instead lose performance relative to an effective static mixture. Predicting which standalone model has the better rank supplies a convenient supervised signal, but converting that prediction directly into a mixture weight can increase the influence of a weaker model. In our four primary evaluation pairs, direct probability weighting improves on the DEV-selected Global baseline in one pair and degrades in three. Global is the pure primary endpoint in three of those pairs. We therefore ask whether a reference-relative correction improves the utility--loss trade-off, allowing that no adjustment may be preferable. The argument proceeds from observed static complementarity, through possible failure of dynamic weighting, to an empirical test of reference-relative correction; it does not presume a dynamic gain.

The standalone-expert Oracle does not close this gap. It picks the better endpoint after observing the gold answer, whereas a deployable policy must infer one weight from observable query information. Different answers to the same observable query can favor different endpoints, but the policy must use the same weight for that query. Moreover, endpoint-winner labels omit the magnitude of RR changes and their value relative to Global. Finite DEV supervision, limited score-summary features and the chosen action map leave estimation and policy restrictions between Oracle opportunity and realized utility. Neither Oracle potential nor a positive static gain proves that a learned policy can exploit complementarity (Section~\ref{sec:complementarity}).

We address this problem with \emph{Anchored Dynamic Correction} (\adc). The method first selects a static reference weight on the development split (DEV). A logistic model then summarizes query-level score geometry into a model-preference signal. Instead of treating that signal as the final mixture weight, \adc\ maps it to a bounded offset from the reference and uses confidence-based fallback to retain the reference on selected queries. This construction separates the choice of a reliable starting solution from the amount of query-specific adjustment. Dynamic ensembling and score-distribution inputs are established by prior work; our object of study is the reference-relative action map, its radius and its adjustment-rejection semantics, as compared in Table~\ref{tab:nearest-methods}. We do not claim that tanh, clipping, shrinkage or rejection is individually new.
We use \emph{conservative} for this testable reference-and-radius construction. It does not mean that few queries change or that reciprocal-rank losses are unlikely. Those outcomes require separate empirical comparisons against named policies and risk measures; Global itself has zero reference-relative harm.

Our experiments evaluate both net utility and harmful corrections, retaining four primary and two additional architecture pairs. MKG-W supplies the primary retrospective evaluation and DB15K a secondary external evaluation, also retrospective. External denotes another dataset, not independence from prior method development. Three of four primary ADC-minus-Global effects have conditional intervals above zero; D-N is slightly negative and both additional-pair intervals span zero. Complete seed/direction results accompany the paired effects. Supplementary studies preserve failed DynaSemble transfers, add healthy training controls, and compare six action families sharing each fitted preference object and 41 DEV configurations. Hard-clipped logit correction slightly exceeds tanh ADC's TEST MRR in every primary pair, while utility--loss trade-offs vary. These studies follow historical TEST inspection and do not constitute fresh blind confirmation. The evidence motivates reference-relative action control but does not identify tanh as uniquely beneficial or establish that the gains justify dual-scorer deployment cost.

This paper makes three contributions:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Diagnosing opportunity versus realized utility.} Six-pair endpoint-winner and Oracle-gap statistics show complementarity, while winner and gold-rank strata link that opportunity to actual ADC gains and losses. Positive Oracle gaps coexist with near-zero or negative correction utility (Tables~\ref{tab:complement-pairs}, \ref{tab:complement-winners} and~\ref{tab:complement-rank_bins}); they do not prove learnable dynamic gains.
\item \textbf{Specifying reference-relative action control.} ADC makes the static reference, permitted displacement and rejection of an adjustment explicit. Equations~\eqref{eq:fallback} and~\eqref{eq:bound} establish exact fallback and bounded score perturbation under the stated grid condition, without an RR-risk guarantee. Table~\ref{tab:nearest-methods} locates this construction among existing dynamic, shrinkage and rejection mechanisms.
\item \textbf{Identifying conditional value and component trade-offs.} Shared-fit, matched-budget DEV/TEST comparisons show that simple clipping can exceed tanh ADC's MRR (Tables~\ref{tab:matched-dev} and~\ref{tab:matched-test}). Frozen radius/fallback counterfactuals locate component-specific gains and sacrifices (Table~\ref{tab:rerun-ablation}); comparisons with healthy dynamic controls expose the utility--loss trade-off (Tables~\ref{tab:claims-main} and~\ref{tab:conservative-fair}). This supports conditional action design, not a uniquely effective tanh map or a universal winner.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Frozen-model fusion and static references.}
Combining existing predictions is an established alternative to changing the underlying representations. Stacked generalization learns a combiner from predictions on examples held out of base-model fitting~\citep{wolpert1992}. In MMKGC, MoSE combines modality-split scores through equal averaging, relation-level boosting or an instance-specific meta-learner~\citep{mose}, so score fusion need not be query-dependent. Our pairs instead comprise independently trained M-Hyper, NativE and AdaMF-MAT scorers; their different fusion and augmentation designs motivate the pair choices, while their representations remain frozen~\citep{mhyper,native,adamf}.

An estimated combiner also needs comparison with a simple reference. In forecast combination, partially egalitarian LASSO selects forecasts and shrinks surviving weights toward equality~\citep{diebold_shin2019}. That work concerns regularized forecast weights, rather than per-query KGC corrections, but establishes shrinkage toward simple combinations as prior art. Our DEV-selected Global need not be an equal mixture. The direct alternative $\alpha=(1-\lambda)\alpha_0+\lambda p_A$ shrinks a learned query weight toward that reference and includes Global at $\lambda=0$. It is therefore a substantive baseline, not a novel ingredient of ADC; matched comparisons test whether a signed bounded correction adds value beyond it (Section~\ref{sec:matched-results}).

\paragraph{Query weights and preference calibration.}
DynaSemble learns query-dependent weights from candidate-score distributions of separately trained textual and structural KGC models~\citep{dynasemble}. Dynamic ensembling and these inputs precede ADC. Table~\ref{tab:nearest-methods} compares the supervision, reference, action space and rejection semantics of the nearest methods. DynaSemble's fixed coefficient is not a radius around a DEV-selected mixture, and MoSE's inference rules do not define a return-to-static confidence gate; numerical rankings can nevertheless coincide with static or single-model rankings. MoSE is a literature comparison here, with no new performance run. Healthy DynaSemble controls test dynamic competition, while shared-fit Query-soft, shrinkage and clipped maps isolate action design from input and learner changes.

Probability calibration asks whether reported confidence agrees with the frequency of the event being predicted~\citep{guo2017}. Here that event is the standalone endpoint winner, not a beneficial adjustment relative to Global. Even a calibrated winner probability would omit the magnitude and sign of the proposed correction's RR change. ADC's class-balanced logistic output is used as a preference signal; neither it nor $|2p_A-1|$ is validated as a probability of harmless adjustment. The threshold is selected for DEV MRR, not calibrated to a risk tolerance.
\input{tables/complementarity/nearest.tex}

\paragraph{Rejecting adjustments and defining risk.}
SelectiveNet jointly learns prediction and rejection, assessing risk on the covered domain~\citep{selectivenet}. ADC's fallback still returns the reference ranking for every rejected adjustment, so adjustment frequency is not prediction coverage. Shrink-gate and Clip-g share this gate. We assess RR utility and loss over all queries relative to Global, plus harm frequency and conditional loss; the action bound supplies no probability guarantee (Section~\ref{sec:conservative-definition}).

Evaluation must also separate fitting, selection and reporting. Cawley and Talbot show that optimizing a finite-sample selection criterion can itself overfit and bias performance comparisons~\citep{cawley2010}. Our grouped held-out DEV comparisons separate combiner fitting and tuning from fold assessment, and the feature/filter boundary separates observable inputs from answer-aware evaluation. These controls answer different questions from historical TEST independence: the recorded design history supports retrospective comparisons, not fresh confirmation (Appendices~\ref{app:information-boundary} and~\ref{app:test-history}).

\section{Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:problem}

Let a knowledge graph contain triples $(h,r,t)$ over entities $\mathcal E$ and relations $\mathcal R$, with modality features attached to entities. A tail query is $(h,r,?)$ and a head query is $(?,r,t)$. Two independently trained, frozen scoring models produce $s_p(q,e)$ and $s_s(q,e)$ for each candidate $e\in\mathcal E$. The subscripts denote the primary and secondary models. We orient all scores so that a larger score indicates greater plausibility.

\paragraph{Primary selection.}
We select the primary using standalone DEV MRR. The evaluated \emph{reliable-primary} regime requires a positive pooled primary-minus-secondary DEV difference, a positive difference in each of three paired seeds, and a positive lower bound of an original-triple clustered bootstrap 95\% interval. All six evaluated MKG-W/DB15K pairs meet these criteria. This definition establishes the empirical reliability asymmetry used to organize the experiments.

\paragraph{Score normalization and combination.}
Let $\widetilde s_p$ and $\widetilde s_s$ be query-wise standardized candidate scores. Each model uses its own unfiltered finite set $F_m(q)=\{e\in\mathcal E:s_m(q,e)\text{ is finite}\}$; entity IDs align, but these sets need not coincide. For $n_m=|F_m(q)|>0$, define $\mu_m=n_m^{-1}\sum_{e\in F_m}s_m(q,e)$ and the population standard deviation $\sigma_m=[n_m^{-1}\sum_{e\in F_m}(s_m(q,e)-\mu_m)^2]^{1/2}$. For $e\in F_m$, the transformation is
\begin{equation}
\widetilde s_m(q,e)=\frac{s_m(q,e)-\mu_m(q)}{\sigma_m(q)+\epsilon},
\qquad \epsilon=10^{-8},
\label{eq:zscore}
\end{equation}
where $\epsilon=10^{-8}$ is added after the square root, not inside the variance or as a lower clamp. Non-finite entries become $-\infty$; a constant finite support becomes zero on that support, and an empty support becomes an all-$-\infty$ row. Neither the designated gold nor the evaluation fact index enters these statistics. Known-answer filtering is applied only to evaluation copies after normalization; exact standalone endpoint ranks are preserved. Appendix~\ref{app:normalization} gives the pseudocode, floating-point and abnormal-row limitations. We define a mixture by
\begin{equation}
s_\alpha(q,e)=\alpha\widetilde s_p(q,e)+(1-\alpha)\widetilde s_s(q,e),
\quad \alpha\in[0,1].
\label{eq:mixture}
\end{equation}
Throughout the paper, $\alpha$ is the \emph{primary weight}; $\alpha=1$ recovers the primary model.

\paragraph{Reference-relative utility.}
Given a static reference $\alpha_0$, we assess a dynamic policy by its query-level reciprocal-rank change
\begin{equation}
\Delta_i=\RR_{\mathrm{policy},i}-\RR_{\alpha_0,i}.
\label{eq:delta}
\end{equation}
An observation $i$ identifies an original triple, a paired model seed, and a prediction direction. A positive mean $\Delta_i$ indicates useful adaptation; a negative individual $\Delta_i$ is a harmful correction. This formulation makes average utility and the incidence of harm separate experimental quantities.

\section{Anchored Dynamic Correction}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{DEV-selected static reference}
\adc\ begins with a Global weight selected by exact filtered DEV MRR:
\begin{equation}
\alpha_0\in\arg\max_{\alpha\in\Agrid}\MRR_{\mathrm{DEV}}(s_\alpha),
\qquad \Agrid=\{0,0.05,\ldots,1\}.
\label{eq:anchor}
\end{equation}
The reference can be an interior mixture or a standalone endpoint. Its role is operational: the policy retains it whenever adaptation is rejected, and all changes are expressed relative to it. Figure~\ref{fig:method} summarizes the resulting pipeline.

\subsection{A winner-supervised preference signal}
\label{sec:signal}
We describe each query with 13 score-geometry features: prediction direction; each model's top-1 score, top-5 mean, top-1/top-2 margin, and score standard deviation; and the four corresponding primary-minus-secondary differences. These statistics summarize the concentration and disagreement of the models' candidate distributions. In the corrected protocol, feature extraction accepts only the two unfiltered score matrices and the prediction direction; it has no gold, reference-score, or filter-index input.

On DEV, we construct the binary target
\begin{equation}
y_i=\ind[\RR_{p,i}>\RR_{s,i}],
\label{eq:label}
\end{equation}
excluding equal-RR observations from classifier fitting. A median imputer and standard scaler precede a balanced logistic regression. With transformed features $x(q)$, the decision and probability are
\begin{equation}
g(q)=w^\top x(q)+b,
\qquad p_A(q)=\sigma(g(q)).
\label{eq:signal}
\end{equation}
The classifier has 13 coefficients and one intercept: 14 trainable selector parameters, excluding preprocessing state and both frozen scorers. The four difference coordinates are linear combinations of the eight expert statistics on complete inputs in exact arithmetic, so these are not 13 independent signals. They change the effective L2 regularization geometry after scaling, rather than expanding the affine prediction space; independent imputation can break the dependency (Appendix~\ref{app:feature-dimension}). This count does not measure feature-extraction or end-to-end cost. Its training target represents standalone model preference; the policy mapping determines how that preference changes the reference mixture. DEV answers supply supervision, while inference uses candidate-distribution statistics and direction.

\subsection{Bounded adjustment and fallback}
The preference signal produces the raw bounded proposal
\begin{equation}
\alpha_{\mathrm{raw}}(q)=\clip\!\left(\alpha_0+\beta\tanh(g(q)),0,1\right),
\label{eq:raw}
\end{equation}
where $\beta$ controls the radius of adjustment around the reference. We use the confidence score $c(q)=|2p_A(q)-1|$ to decide whether to apply the proposal:
\begin{equation}
\alpha_{\mathrm{cont}}(q)=
\begin{cases}
\alpha_0, & c(q)<\tau\ \text{or the feature vector is non-finite},\\
\alpha_{\mathrm{raw}}(q), & \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\label{eq:fallback}
\end{equation}
For finite logits, the gate and proposed magnitude use the same signal:
\begin{equation}
c=|\tanh(g/2)|,\qquad |\beta\tanh(g)|=\beta\frac{2c}{1+c^2}.
\label{eq:confidence-displacement}
\end{equation}
Thus a retained query with $c\geq\tau$ has an unclipped proposed displacement of at least $2\beta\tau/(1+\tau^2)$. Increasing $\tau$ selects larger absolute logits; it does not rescale the proposal of a query retained under both thresholds. Confidence selection and proposed magnitude are therefore coupled. Clipping can reduce actual displacement, including to zero, and grid projection further changes it; the formula is not a general lower bound on realized weight movement or ranking benefit.

The final applied weight is $\widehat\alpha(q)=\Pi_{\Agrid}(\alpha_{\mathrm{cont}}(q))$. Projection selects the nearest grid value, breaking equal distances first toward the anchor and then toward the smaller weight. The 0.05 grid is part of the reported algorithm: around a grid-aligned anchor it suppresses proposals within approximately 0.025, adding an action dead zone beyond the confidence gate. It also enables reuse of precomputed exact full-entity ranks. Projection changes the magnitude of surviving adjustments as well, so its ranking effect requires measurement. The fixed sensitivity comparison in Appendix~\ref{app:grid-sensitivity} retains three positive and one negative primary TEST effect relative to Global under finer and continuous weights, while revealing resolution-dependent utility and loss.

The original DEV search uses $\beta\in\{0.05,0.10,\ldots,0.50\}$ and $\tau\in\{0,0.1,0.2,0.3\}$. The selected pair maximizes DEV MRR; ties favor smaller $\beta$ and then larger $\tau$. The model, preprocessing, reference, grids, and selected parameters are serialized before TEST application. The matched supplement adds a single explicit $\beta=0$ policy, called ADC + Global, which returns the reference for every query regardless of confidence. This extends the original 40 configurations to 41; the original results remain identifiable. Algorithm~\ref{alg:policy} gives the inference procedure.

\subsection{A testable meaning of conservative}
\label{sec:conservative-definition}
The policy is \emph{reference-anchored} because fallback returns exactly to a fixed DEV-selected $\alpha_0$, and \emph{bounded-action} because every applied weight satisfies $|\widehat\alpha(q)-\alpha_0|\leq\beta$ for the evaluated, grid-aligned radii. These are deterministic properties of the action rule. They do not bound the fraction of changed weights or the probability or magnitude of RR harm. We reserve claims of lower empirical harm for specified comparators and metrics.
Let $d(q,e)=\widetilde s_p(q,e)-\widetilde s_s(q,e)$. The mixture admits the identity
\begin{equation}
s_\alpha(q,e)-s_{\alpha_0}(q,e)=(\alpha-\alpha_0)d(q,e).
\label{eq:identity}
\end{equation}

\begin{proposition}[Bounded candidate-score perturbation]
For a candidate whose two normalized scores are finite, the continuous policy in Eq.~\eqref{eq:fallback} satisfies
\begin{equation}
\left|s_{\alpha_{\mathrm{cont}}}(q,e)-s_{\alpha_0}(q,e)\right|
\leq\beta|d(q,e)|.
\label{eq:bound}
\end{equation}
The same inequality holds after nearest-grid projection when the clipped interval endpoints $\max(0,\alpha_0-\beta)$ and $\min(1,\alpha_0+\beta)$ lie on the grid, as in the evaluated configurations.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The magnitude of $\beta\tanh(g)$ is at most $\beta$. Projection onto $[0,1]$ cannot increase distance from $\alpha_0\in[0,1]$, and fallback sets that distance to zero. Grid-aligned interval endpoints ensure that the nearest grid point remains inside the interval. Applying Eq.~\eqref{eq:identity} completes the proof.
\end{proof}

This elementary property follows from the linear-mixture identity and $|\tanh(g)|\leq1$; it documents the action constraint rather than supplying a new ranking-risk theorem. The bound gives $\beta$ a direct interpretation in score space: it limits movement in proportion to inter-model disagreement. It is not an absolute, data-independent score bound, since $d$ varies with the scores. Candidate ordering also depends on the reference margins. An arbitrarily small perturbation can reverse a near tie and reduce a gold answer's RR from 1 to $1/2$. Appendix~\ref{app:bound} gives a sufficient margin condition for preserving an ordering. Without such margin information, this property supplies neither a nontrivial RR-loss bound tending to zero with $\beta$ nor a probabilistic harm guarantee. Non-finite normalized entries fall outside the finite-score algebra.

\paragraph{Filtering scope and information boundary.}
The default shared evaluator builds known-answer sets from TRAIN$\cup$DEV$\cup$TEST for both DEV and TEST evaluation, including the historical ADC/R0 development evaluations. This is evaluation-side access to known facts, not use of TEST triples as positive training examples. Base-model supervision and training negative filters use TRAIN only. New DynaSemble controls instead use the strict DEV loader, which does not open TEST and evaluates DEV with TRAIN$\cup$DEV. Their inner-fold negative sampler uses TRAIN$\cup$DEV-fit; final refitting uses TRAIN$\cup$DEV. All methods evaluate TEST with the full fact union. Thus new and historical DEV scores have different filtering scopes and are not directly comparable. Neither evaluation filter enters inference features or candidate normalization. The current gold is always retained while other known answers are excluded from ranking. Details and pseudocode appear in Appendix~\ref{app:information-boundary}.

\subsection{Comparison methods}
\paragraph{Standalone and static policies.}
We report both standalone models, equal query-wise z-score averaging, equal reciprocal-rank fusion (RRF, $k=60$), Global, and Relation in Table~\ref{tab:matched-static}. Relation selects a relation-specific weight on DEV, with the shared weight used for low-support relations. RRF adds $1/(60+r_p(e))+1/(60+r_s(e))$ from the complete unfiltered candidate competition ranks, then filters the combined ranking. Global is the reference for every correction metric. The expert-selection Oracle, $\max(\RR_p,\RR_s)$ per observation, appears separately in Table~\ref{tab:matched-oracle}. It uses answers, is not deployable, and is not an upper bound on score fusion: a mixture can rank the gold above both standalone experts.

\paragraph{Dynamic policies.}
Query-soft applies $\alpha(q)=p_A(q)$ using exactly the same fitted 13-feature logistic object and output probabilities as ADC, not a separately fitted classifier. This holds for each original full-geometry OOF fit and the final serialized DEV model. DynaSemble~\citep{dynasemble} uses four distribution features with query-wise min-max scores, rather than ADC's z-scores. R0 retains the historical three-seed transfer, including its failed seeds and identified sampled-training-feature and initialization-order deviations. R1 aligns full-distribution training features and initialization order with the pinned public core. R2 changes output initialization to keep the initial ReLU active. R3-fixed replaces the final ReLU with Softplus while retaining the one-epoch, $5\times10^{-5}$ learning-rate budget. R3 selects learning rate and epoch using grouped DEV cross-validation. R4 swaps the learned and fixed coefficients, inheriting R3's configuration without further tuning. Each new variant reports all nine base--selector seed combinations. Appendix~\ref{app:dynasemble} details the differences and results. These are transparent transfers to new scorer pairs, not reproductions of the original paper's datasets or model combinations.

\paragraph{Matched action alternatives.}
The supplementary comparison shares each fitted preference object, its $g,p_A$, the anchor, outer folds, normalized scores and ranking grid across six families. Shrink uses $\alpha=(1-\lambda)\alpha_0+\lambda p_A$, including $\lambda=0$ (Global) and $\lambda=1$ (Query-soft). Shrink-gate also tunes the same confidence fallback as ADC. Linear-p replaces $\tanh(g)$ by $2p_A-1$, and Clip-g replaces it by $\clip(g,-1,1)$, retaining the local radius. Linear-g uses a scaled logit with only $[0,1]$ clipping, so it does not have the same local-radius bound. Each family, including ADC + Global, receives 41 DEV configurations and explicit Global. Appendix~\ref{app:matched} specifies the grids and budget allocation; DynaSemble is not treated as a mapping-only control.

\paragraph{Component variants.}
\emph{Expanded-radius} fixes $\beta=1$ while retaining the full policy's fitted signal, $\alpha_0$, threshold, tanh mapping, $[0,1]$ clipping, grid and non-finite fallback. It expands the local radius; it is not a freely parameterized dynamic weight. At an interior anchor, the logit's sign still determines the proposal's direction and tanh still determines its magnitude before clipping and projection. \emph{No fallback} fixes $\tau=0$ while retaining $\alpha_0$, $\beta$, and the model; non-finite features still return to the reference. These variants inherit the corresponding full policy settings without additional TEST selection. Query-soft changes the complete mapping from preference to action, including the reference-relative construction and confidence fallback.

\subsection{DEV fitting and TEST application}
We evaluate the policy family with five-fold grouped cross-fitting on DEV. An original triple is the grouping unit, and all three seeds and both directions remain in one fold. Each outer training fold selects its Global weight, fits its feature preprocessing and logistic classifier, and selects $\beta$ and $\tau$ using only that training portion. Performance is measured on held-out triples. The formal expanded-policy DEV evaluation is reported in Appendix~\ref{app:dev}.

After this evaluation, the classifier is refitted on full DEV and the final reference and policy parameters are locked. TEST applies the serialized policy without refitting. The deployed radii are 0.45, 0.50, 0.20, and 0.50 for W-N, W-A, D-N, and D-A; thresholds are 0.1, 0.1, 0.2, and 0.3. Corresponding primary-weight anchors are 0.55, 1, 1, and 1. The exact feature order and selection rules appear in Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}.
The upper limit 0.50 is a historical design restriction on the searched action family. There is no documented calibration connecting it to a tolerable RR loss or harm probability. Selection maximizes DEV MRR within that family, not over all radii. To examine the restriction, a supplementary analysis reconstructs all 20 original fold models and verifies their selected parameters and held-out predictions before comparing caps 0.50 and 1.00. Both families choose $\beta,\tau$ using training-portion MRR, with identical classifiers, anchors and tie-breaking. Held-out DEV outcomes assess the resulting trade-off. The wider family and full radius curves do not choose any new TEST policy (Appendix~\ref{app:radius-review}). This analysis follows historical TEST inspection and is descriptive.
The matched action supplement follows the same outer-fold selection procedure, then locks all 24 full-DEV family configurations before a separate TEST application phase. Within $10^{-12}$ of maximal selection MRR, it prefers explicit Global, then smaller strength and larger threshold. No family-specific learner fitting or early stopping is introduced. All candidate scores, duplicate action-vector counts, final choices and held-out results are retained. Adding Global guarantees that the best selection score is no worse than that candidate up to the tie tolerance; it does not guarantee nonnegative held-out or TEST utility.

\subsection{Metrics and statistical analysis}
We report exact filtered MRR and Hits@1/3/10. For the change $\Delta_i$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:delta}, harm rate and benefit rate are the fractions of all evaluated observations with negative and positive changes. Conditional mean harm and benefit quantify their magnitudes:
\begin{align}
H&=N^{-1}\sum_i\ind[\Delta_i<0], & B&=N^{-1}\sum_i\ind[\Delta_i>0],\\
C&=\E[-\Delta_i\mid\Delta_i<0], & G&=\E[\Delta_i\mid\Delta_i>0].
\end{align}
These quantities yield the decomposition
\begin{equation}
U=\Delta\MRR=L_+-L_-,\qquad L_-=\E[\max(-\Delta_i,0)]=H C,\quad L_+=B G.
\label{eq:utility-harm}
\end{equation}
The unconditional mean loss $L_-$ counts every observation, including zeros, and jointly reflects harm frequency and severity. Conditional means are undefined when their event is empty; Global has $U=L_-=H=0$ and undefined $C$. For policies using the same z-score coefficient coordinate, we report weight-change frequency $I_\alpha=\Pr(\widehat\alpha\ne\alpha_0)$ and mean displacement $D_\alpha=\E|\widehat\alpha-\alpha_0|$. DynaSemble's min-max coefficient ratio cannot be subtracted directly from the z-score Global weight; its $I_\alpha,D_\alpha$ are omitted. The common outcome-change measure $J=\Pr(\Delta_i\ne0)$ is the gold-answer rank-change rate, not a full-order change rate. All probabilities and expectations here are empirical averages over the declared observations.

The main \adc\ effect intervals use 10,000 percentile clustered-bootstrap resamples with seed 20260910. Each resampling unit is an original triple containing all six base-seed--direction observations. New DynaSemble controls first average reciprocal ranks across selector repetitions within each base-seed--direction observation, then use the same original-triple clusters. Harm rates use actual selector observations before this averaging. Pair-level intervals quantify triple-sampling uncertainty conditional on the fitted models and observed seeds, not uncertainty from retraining, historical TEST reuse or adaptive method, feature and pair selection. They are descriptive conditional intervals rather than confirmatory evidence for the development process. They are pointwise intervals without correction for multiple comparisons. Complete seed/direction matrices supplement the pooled results.
The supplementary loss and intervention comparisons also use actual selector observations before averaging: otherwise opposite-signed changes could cancel and understate loss. They add no confidence intervals or statistical risk constraints; any dominance statement concerns observed point estimates.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Why the reference matters.}
A fixed reference separates established performance from additional intervention. At the primary endpoint, direct probability weighting can introduce substantial secondary influence. Comparisons sharing the exact fitted preference object isolate the action family from features and learner changes. They show that ADC can outperform ungated shrinkage on some pairs, while simple hard-clipped correction can match or exceed its accuracy. The evidence concerns reference-relative policy design and its trade-offs, not a uniquely effective tanh function or the isolated contribution of the anchor. Equal configuration counts still leave different attainable action sets and different numbers of duplicate grid policies.
At $\alpha_0=1$, shrinkage with $\lambda>0$ moves below the primary endpoint whenever $p<1$, even when the signal favors the primary ($p>1/2$). In contrast, centered tanh, linear-probability and clipped-logit proposals remain at that endpoint after clipping whenever $g\geq0$. This algebra distinguishes shrinkage toward a probability from a signed reference-relative correction; the distinction does not require tanh.

\paragraph{Preference supervision and correction utility.}
The classifier learns an endpoint-winner label, not RR improvement over Global. The label omits effect magnitude, so even correct endpoint preference does not ensure a beneficial mixture adjustment. A gold-aware Oracle also bypasses the inference problem faced by a policy using only observable score summaries and direction. Finite DEV samples can introduce estimation error, the feature representation may omit relevant distinctions, and the bounded/grid-projected action map restricts attainable mixtures. These are distinct reasons why Oracle or static complementarity need not become dynamic utility; this study does not decompose their contributions. Exploitation is assessed through actual grouped held-out DEV and locked TEST effects relative to Global, retaining their retrospective status and negative results.

\paragraph{Meaning of conservative adaptation.}
Conservative denotes the reference-anchored, bounded-action rule in Section~\ref{sec:conservative-definition}. It does not mean rare or harmless intervention: W-N changes 63.33\% of weights and harms 15.150\% of observations. The radius limits coefficient movement and the associated score perturbation; empirical ranking losses remain separate. ADC reduces selected loss measures relative to the frozen expanded-radius policy and healthy R3, but it cannot dominate Global's zero loss and does not dominate Query-soft on every W-N risk measure. The 0.50 cap restricts an MRR-optimized DEV family without an explicit risk tolerance. Wider DEV selection improves utility in three pairs at increased unconditional loss, which supplies a trade-off rather than a uniquely justified optimum. Neither this analysis nor preference confidence provides a probabilistic safety guarantee.

\paragraph{MMKGC as an application setting.}
The known-entity coverage diagnostic uses only the observed side of each query; it does not look up the unknown gold entity's modality attributes. Its heterogeneous outcomes do not support a uniform missing-modality benefit: W-A's image-only TEST group is negative while its both-supported group is positive, whereas D-N's both-supported group is negative. Availability flags do not measure semantic quality, and relation/entity composition can confound these associations. We have not performed a causal modality-removal experiment or shown that the asymmetry is uniquely multimodal. ADC therefore remains a general score-level construction evaluated on multimodal scorers, rather than a modality-specific adaptation mechanism.

\paragraph{Generalization and computation.}
The evidence covers two main evaluation datasets, three base architectures, three paired seeds, and six model pairs, with the historical OpenBG numerical results excluded from this corrected draft. The reliable-primary rule holds in all six main and additional pairs, so its necessity for successful correction remains an open question. It is not sufficient: D-N meets the rule but has slightly negative held-out DEV and TEST utility (Tables~\ref{tab:matched-dev} and~\ref{tab:claims-intervals}). A favorable endpoint gap therefore defines the studied regime, not a reason by itself to deploy ADC. Both datasets have the retrospective status described in Section~\ref{sec:setup}; DB15K is a second-dataset external evaluation, not an independent replication. Historical TEST influence on the choice of method, features and pairs cannot be ruled out by the available records. Parameter serialization, grouped DEV checks and the historical 77-check audit cannot restore holdout independence. Fresh confirmation would require a complete, traceable design freeze before inspecting an evaluation set still unexposed to that design. Inference requires both scorers even for queries that eventually fall back, since the policy uses their candidate distributions. Efficient feature extraction and score reuse are therefore important practical directions.

\section{Conclusion}
Across four primary pairs and two additional architecture pairs on MKG-W and DB15K, the retrospective evidence supports conditional gains from reference-relative correction. Three primary ADC-minus-Global conditional intervals lie above zero; D-N is slightly negative and its interval and both additional-pair intervals span zero (Table~\ref{tab:claims-intervals}). Healthy DynaSemble has higher MRR in two primary pairs, and Relation also exceeds ADC on W-N. ADC has lower unconditional RR loss relative to Global than healthy DynaSemble in the four primary pairs, while Global itself has zero such loss. Matched hard-clipped correction has slightly higher MRR in all four primary pairs. The contribution is an explicit action constraint with measured trade-offs, without evidence of a unique tanh advantage.

Anchored Dynamic Correction offers a bounded way to adjust an effective static mixture when two frozen scorers already exist and one is reliably stronger on DEV. In that setting it is worth considering only when held-out DEV gains justify the associated RR losses and the cost of obtaining both score arrays; retaining Global or choosing a stronger control remains a valid outcome. A small selector does not make dual scoring cheap: historical pre-repair timing showed substantially greater cost than primary-only, and corrected end-to-end timing remains unmeasured. The action bound is not an RR-risk guarantee. These findings do not establish benefit for pairs without a reliable primary, under distribution shift, or from modality-specific adaptation, and the retrospective design leaves independent confirmation open.

\subsection{Exact score normalization and exceptional inputs}
\label{app:normalization}
The export module is \texttt{scripts/\allowbreak eval\_\allowbreak heterogeneous\_\allowbreak complementarity.py}. Candidate construction uses \texttt{score\_expert\_block}. The unfiltered rows pass to \texttt{query\_zscore\_with\_reference}, followed by \texttt{filtered\_copy} and \texttt{endpoint\_safe\_mixed\_ranks}. The candidate output agrees with \texttt{normalize\_candidate\_scores} in \texttt{query\_zscore} mode; the separate generic \texttt{combine\_expert\_scores} helper uses a different finite-fill rule and is not this paper's ranking path.
For each model and each unfiltered row $s$, compute:
\begin{quote}\small
$f_e\leftarrow\operatorname{isfinite}(s_e)$, $n\leftarrow\max(1,\sum_e f_e)$.\\
$\mu\leftarrow\sum_e\operatorname{where}(f_e,s_e,0)/n$.\\
$v_e\leftarrow\operatorname{where}(f_e,s_e-\mu,0)$.\\
$\sigma\leftarrow\sqrt{\sum_e v_e^2/n}$, $a\leftarrow\sigma+10^{-8}$.\\
$z_e\leftarrow\operatorname{where}(f_e,v_e/a,-\infty)$.\\
Only in evaluation: $z_y^{\rm ref}\leftarrow(s_y^{\rm ref}-\mu)/a$, then filter owned candidate copies while retaining the current gold.
\end{quote}
NaN, $+\infty$ and $-\infty$ are all excluded from the moments and become $-\infty$ in $z$. The finite sets are computed independently, not intersected or united across experts. With finite moment arithmetic in the evaluated float32 dtype, one finite value or any constant finite support gives $\sigma=0$ and finite entries map to zero. With no finite values the clamped count gives $\mu=\sigma=0$ internally, but every output remains $-\infty$. The candidate normalization is row-wise and invariant to which gold or other batch rows are supplied. Arithmetic retains the incoming tensor dtype; it does not upcast moments, so sufficiently extreme finite values can overflow. Lower-precision dtypes are not covered by this numerical audit.

For an interior weight $0<\alpha<1$, the actual ranking path directly interpolates normalized copies: a candidate missing from either expert's finite support is therefore at $-\infty$. Shared answer masks also remain $-\infty$. At weights 0 and 1, the path explicitly substitutes the original expert rank, avoiding $0\cdot(-\infty)$ and preserving endpoint evaluation. A separately scored gold is transformed using the same moments, not included as an extra sample.

These rules define normalization even for degenerate rows, but they do not provide reliable abnormal-input recovery. The legacy gold transform and strict-greater rank function do not reject non-finite references: an all-non-finite row with NaN gold can return rank 1 because every comparison is false. Such output is not valid accuracy evidence. An independent test records this counterexample. The retained feature tables alone cannot certify finite raw scores, because geometry sanitization can hide non-finite scores.

A completed frozen-checkpoint re-execution audits all 18 checkpoints (two datasets, three architectures and three seeds), both DEV/TEST splits and both prediction directions: 72 cells and 474,732 expert/query scoring rows, including repetitions across models, seeds and directions. It finds zero non-finite candidate or gold scores, empty or constant finite supports, non-finite moments, or invalid normalized scores. The returned checkpoint/configuration hashes match the existing baseline records, and all four ordered splits are independently reconstructed from the bound split ledgers. The source-bound raw-score review accompanies the supplement. These findings support the finite-input conditions for this re-execution and indicate no anomaly-driven reevaluation; they do not establish bitwise equality to historical raw scores that were not retained, or robustness to arbitrary abnormal inputs. No training, policy selection or replacement of historical ranks is performed.

\paragraph{Features.}
The 13-dimensional vector is ordered as follows: tail-direction indicator; primary top-1, top-5 mean, top-1/top-2 margin, and score standard deviation; the corresponding four secondary statistics; then primary-minus-secondary differences of these four statistics. In the corrected implementation, geometry is extracted from an owned copy of the unfiltered all-entity score matrix before both masking and mixture normalization. The geometry standard deviation uses the sample correction; mixture z-score uses population variance. The feature routine sanitizes non-finite model outputs for computing statistics; evaluation-mask sentinels are not permitted inputs. The policy separately flags non-finite resulting feature vectors for fallback. Tied standalone RR labels are excluded from fitting.

\subsection{Gold and filtering boundary}
\label{app:information-boundary}
For tail prediction define $K(q)=\{e:(h,r,e)\in F\}$, and for head prediction define $K(q)=\{e:(e,r,t)\in F\}$, where $F$ is the declared evaluation fact union. Only ranking uses $K(q)\setminus\{y\}$ for the current gold $y$. A separately scored gold reference is transformed using statistics of the unfiltered candidate distribution and is never a selector input. Ranking uses $1+\sum_e\ind[s(q,e)>s(q,y)]$ (strict-greater ties); endpoint weights reuse the standalone expert ranks.

Head scoring calls each model's directional interface. M-Hyper maps $(e,r,t)$ to $(t,r^{-1},e)$ internally, with inverse relation ID $r+|\mathcal R|$ for a base relation. NativE and AdaMF-MAT directly score $(e,r,t)$. The head filter remains indexed by the original $(r,t)$ and candidate head IDs in all cases. Canonical preprocessing rejects within-split duplicate triples and exact cross-split overlaps. Filter indexes are sets, so repeated facts have no additional masking effect. The low-level reader and evaluator do not silently deduplicate observations; bypassing canonical preprocessing would retain their multiplicity.

\begin{quote}\small
\textbf{Inference (no gold or filter input):}\\
1. Score every entity for the observable query with both frozen experts.\\
2. Extract geometry and compute normalization statistics from the full unfiltered score vectors.\\
3. Apply the frozen selector, bound, fallback, and weight grid to obtain $\alpha(q)$.\\
4. Combine the normalized scores (preserve the original expert at endpoints).\\[3pt]
\textbf{Evaluation only:}\\
5. Copy the candidate scores; mask $K(q)\setminus\{y\}$, always retaining $y$.\\
6. Score/transform the gold reference separately and compute its strict-greater rank.\\[3pt]
\textbf{DEV supervision only:}\\
7. Form preference labels and selection metrics from DEV filtered ranks; fit preprocessing and the selector on the designated DEV training fold. TEST never refits them.
\end{quote}
Masking normalized expert copies before a fixed row-wise interpolation is equivalent to masking the combined scores for the shared filter. The copies cannot flow back into geometry or normalization. Regression tests vary the designated gold and the fact index through the actual export path and require identical geometry and policy weights for an unchanged observable query, while allowing the evaluation rank to change. The completed rerun covers six MKG-W/DB15K pairs and four retrained DynaSemble transfers. Across all six pairs, deployed ADC and Query-soft grid weights are exactly equal within every multi-gold observable-query group in both DEV and TEST. Some exported geometry and continuous outputs differ slightly across repeated observations: the largest ADC continuous-weight span is $4.21\times10^{-7}$ and the largest DynaSemble learned-weight span is $2.39\times10^{-7}$. These observations are reported separately from exact grid-weight invariance. Controlled gold/filter intervention tests pass, but the exports alone do not establish the cause of these numerical differences or bitwise repeatability under arbitrary batching. No post-hoc averaging across golds was applied.

\paragraph{Classifier.}
The implementation uses median imputation, feature standardization, and \texttt{LogisticRegression} with \texttt{class\_weight=balanced}, \texttt{solver=liblinear}, \texttt{max\_iter=2000}, and default $C=1$. The final model random state is 20260902; outer-fold fits use their recorded deterministic offsets. Fitted imputation and scaling statistics are reused on held-out and TEST observations. Confidence is the absolute distance of the predicted preference probability from 0.5, scaled to $[0,1]$.

The geometry standard-deviation coordinate uses \texttt{torch.std} with sample correction 1, unlike population normalization above. Top-5 means use $\min(5,|\mathcal E|)$ candidates; geometry requires at least two candidates. Its existing sanitizer uses the finite minimum/maximum over the entire supplied expert batch, with low=min$-1$ and high=max$+1$, or $-100,100$ when the batch has no finite values; NaN/$-\infty$ become low and $+\infty$ becomes high. Thus abnormal geometry can depend on other batch rows even though normalization does not. At classifier input, remaining non-finite coordinates are converted to NaN for column-median imputation and separately flagged for Global fallback. That flag does not detect raw anomalies already sanitized upstream. No batch-invariance or safe-fallback claim is made for such abnormal inputs.

Five-fold assignments use deterministic relation-stratified SHA-256 ordering and round-robin allocation of original triples, with fold seed 20260901. The six observations of a triple share a fold. The outer training data supply the static anchor, classifier, and training-portion MRR used to select the bounded policy. This is selection within each outer training fold; it does not introduce an additional inner cross-validation loop. The final full-DEV fit selects from the same expanded grids and serializes all parameters and source hashes.

\paragraph{Definitions and inference boundary.}
For observation $i$, let $a_i=\RR_{A,i}$ and $b_i=\RR_{B,i}$, with A the previously designated DEV primary. Endpoint winner fractions are the empirical frequencies of $a_i>b_i$, $b_i>a_i$ and equality. We report
\begin{equation}
O=\E[\max(a_i,b_i)]-\max\{\E[a_i],\E[b_i]\},\qquad
D_r=\E\left[\left|\log_2\frac{r_{A,i}}{r_{B,i}}\right|\right].
\label{eq:complementarity}
\end{equation}
The best single is chosen once for the displayed population, whereas the diagnostic Oracle uses the per-observation better endpoint. The identity $\E[\max(a_i,b_i)]-\E[a_i]=\E[(b_i-a_i)_+]$ relates endpoint opportunity to the magnitude of secondary wins. \section{Additional Bounds and Diagnostic Definitions}
\label{app:bound}
For two candidates $e_1,e_2$ with finite normalized scores from both experts, define the reference margin
\begin{equation}
m_0=s_{\alpha_0}(q,e_1)-s_{\alpha_0}(q,e_2).
\end{equation}
The change in this margin is $(\alpha-\alpha_0)[d(q,e_1)-d(q,e_2)]$. Consequently, if
\begin{equation}
m_0>\beta|d(q,e_1)-d(q,e_2)|,
\label{eq:margin-sufficient}
\end{equation}
the ordering $e_1\succ e_2$ is preserved for the evaluated bounded policy. This is a sufficient certificate, not a necessary condition: failing it does not imply that the realized correction changes the order. To certify preservation of the reference top candidate by this argument, it suffices to check the inequality against every alternative; this does not certify the rest of the ranking. Preserving every strict pairwise order requires the corresponding conditions for all such pairs. No claim is made that these conditions hold for every query. For grids whose interval endpoints are not aligned, nearest-grid projection adds at most half the grid spacing to the distance bound; the present spacing is 0.05.

\paragraph{Limits of the bound.}
A regression test constructs normalized score vectors with a near-tied gold and competitor, then verifies that a weight displacement of $10^{-6}$ respects the score bound yet changes gold RR from 1 to $1/2$. Separate tests match grid projection against the production tie rule, verify Global's zero unconditional loss, and demonstrate why selector repetitions must not be averaged before loss calculation. These check action semantics and metric arithmetic; they do not certify population ranking risk.

\paragraph{Fallback accounting.}
On a query assigned to confidence fallback, compare the raw bounded RR with Global. A lower raw RR contributes to avoided loss, a higher one contributes to sacrificed gain, and an equal one is neutral. The net utility is
\begin{equation}
U_{\mathrm{fb}}=\sum_{i\in\mathcal F}(\RR_{\alpha_0,i}-\RR_{\mathrm{raw},i}),
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal F$ is the confidence-fallback subset. $U_{\mathrm{fb}}/N$ is the full-minus-no-fallback MRR difference. Non-finite fallback remains active in both policies.

\section{Redundant Difference Coordinates and Matched Dimensions}
\label{app:feature-dimension}
Let $x_9$ contain direction and the eight per-expert statistics. In exact arithmetic on complete inputs, $x_{13}=Dx_9$, where $D$ stacks the $9\times9$ identity and four rows computing the corresponding A-minus-B statistics. Thus $w_{13}^{\top}x_{13}+b=(D^{\top}w_{13})^{\top}x_9+b$: the added coordinates do not enlarge the affine function class. After column standardization, write $[z_{13};1]=M[z_9;1]$, including the centering offset. Since $M$ has full column rank, the minimal squared coefficient norm representing an effective augmented 9D coefficient $v$ is
\begin{equation}
\min_{M^{\top}w=v}\|w\|_2^2=v^{\top}(M^{\top}M)^{-1}v.
\label{eq:redundant-penalty}
\end{equation}
This differs from isotropic 9D L2 regularization. The augmented expression includes the unit synthetic intercept penalized by the evaluated liblinear configuration. As a simple unscaled example, features $(a,b,a-b)$ induce a minimum penalty $(2v_a^2+2v_av_b+2v_b^2)/3$, rather than $v_a^2+v_b^2$. These identities explain a possible parameterization effect; they do not attribute every observed MRR difference solely to regularization.

Column-wise median imputation can break the exact dependency: medians of $a,b,a-b$ need not satisfy the subtraction identity. Remaining non-finite input coordinates are imputed during fitting and flagged for fallback during inference. In the four-pair cache used here, all exported 13D DEV/TEST rows are finite, so imputation does not create the observed differences. Stored float32 subtractions leave residuals up to $1.91\times10^{-6}$; collapsing fitted 13D classifiers to nine raw affine coordinates changes audited decisions by at most $3.17\times10^{-7}$. This numerical residue is not evidence of four independent signals, nor does finite exported geometry certify finite raw scores.

Both dimensions use the same median/scaling pipeline, balanced winner-label logistic loss, $C=1$, liblinear solver, maximum 2,000 iterations, training examples, five grouped DEV folds and random states. Each dimension searches the same 41 ADC configurations, including Global; Query-soft uses that dimension's fitted model without extra tuning. All eight full-DEV pair/dimension locks precede the separate TEST application. The experiment uses 44 small model fits and four reused original full-DEV models; convergence takes at most seven iterations. The runtime is scikit-learn 1.7.1 and the original pickles are 1.7.2. Separate DEV-only refits quantify their small numerical differences and reproduce every one of the 42 DEV action vectors (41 ADC candidates plus Query-soft); the original full-DEV/TEST outputs and grouped OOF ADC ranks are also replayed. This documents numerical compatibility without a bitwise cross-version claim.

\paragraph{Coefficient endpoints and role asymmetry.}

Finite $w$ can nevertheless preserve the complete ranking of a finite candidate set if its ordering margins withstand the perturbation. This is distinct from merely obtaining the same gold rank.

Swapping the fixed coefficient is therefore a consequential parameterization diagnostic, not an automatic repair. The same-config experiment does not establish the best independently tuned reverse orientation.
