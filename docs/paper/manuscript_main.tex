\begin{abstract}
Multimodal knowledge graph completion (MMKGC) aims to improve link prediction by incorporating auxiliary textual and visual evidence, yet in product-oriented knowledge graphs visual support is often incomplete and uneven. This paper studies OpenBG-IMG under a unified filtered-ranking protocol and shows that the current split induces a protocol-defined role--modality asymmetry: head-side targets may be image-supported, whereas tail-side targets are effectively image-unavailable. As a result, multimodal gain is not globally reliable, but local and bounded under the current evaluation protocol. To exploit this bounded gain, we study selective activation between a fusion expert and a structural expert under two deployable information boundaries: strict metadata-only query-level routing and score-aware non-answer-aware expert combination. Experiments show that strict clean routing recovers only modest gain, with regression-based clean routing reaching 0.2982 MRR. In contrast, exposing fixed-expert score patterns substantially improves recoverable gain: a learned candidate-level score-aware router reaches 0.3142 MRR, while simple full-ranking score interpolation reaches up to 0.3428 MRR. These results indicate that the main bottleneck is not merely router complexity, but the deployable information boundary under which expert combination is performed.
\end{abstract}

\textbf{Keywords:} multimodal knowledge graph completion; score-aware expert combination; selective activation; score interpolation; incomplete visual support; protocol-aware evaluation

\section{Introduction}
Knowledge graph completion (KGC) aims to infer missing facts from observed triples. Multimodal knowledge graph completion (MMKGC) extends this setting by using textual and visual evidence to enrich entity representations beyond graph structure~\cite{trouillon2016complex,dettmers2018conve,sun2019rotate,liu2019mmkg,xie2017ikrl,mousselly2018multimodal,pezeshkpour2018mkbe}. In product-oriented knowledge graphs, images are naturally expected to help because many entities have visually meaningful attributes.

However, visual support in product-oriented KGs is often incomplete and uneven. Under the current OpenBG-IMG \texttt{paper\_split}, this incompleteness is not merely random missingness. Target position is entangled with modality availability: head-side targets may still be image-supported, whereas tail-side targets are effectively image-unavailable. The protocol therefore creates a role--modality asymmetry in which multimodal gain cannot be assumed to be globally reliable.

This asymmetry changes the main research question. Rather than asking only how to build a stronger fusion module, this paper asks when multimodal evidence should be activated under deployable information constraints. We first diagnose where multimodal gain actually appears, then compare two selective-activation boundaries. The first is strict metadata-only query-level routing, where decisions must be made from legal query-time metadata. The second is score-aware non-answer-aware expert combination, where both fixed experts are scored first and the combiner may use their score, rank, confidence, and disagreement patterns without observing the hidden answer.

Our experiments follow this progression. Under the unified OpenBG-IMG protocol with filtered ranking and \texttt{direction=both}, \texttt{Residual-only} is the strongest internal-family baseline, while fusion-oriented variants retain value only in local protocol-shaped regimes. Strict clean routing can recover statistically supported but modest gain: direction-specific thresholding reaches 0.2974 MRR, and regression-based clean routing reaches 0.2982 MRR. Once non-answer-aware fixed-expert score patterns are available, recoverable gain becomes substantially larger: CA-S2 reaches 0.3142 MRR, and simple full-ranking score interpolation reaches up to 0.3428 MRR.

The paper's claim is therefore controlled. We do not claim to propose a universally stronger MMKGC architecture. Instead, we provide a protocol-aware analysis and selective expert-combination framework for MMKGC under incomplete visual support. The results show that the main bottleneck is not only router architecture complexity, but the deployable information boundary under which expert combination is performed.

We organize the empirical analysis around three research questions. \textbf{RQ1} asks whether multimodal gain is globally reliable under the OpenBG-IMG protocol. \textbf{RQ2} asks how much bounded gain strict metadata-only routing can recover. \textbf{RQ3} asks whether score-aware non-answer-aware expert combination recovers substantially more deployable gain.

This paper makes three contributions:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em]
    \item \textbf{Protocol-aware bounded-gain diagnosis.} We show that the current OpenBG-IMG protocol induces role--modality asymmetry, which makes multimodal gain local and bounded rather than globally reliable.
    \item \textbf{Selective activation under strict clean information boundaries.} We evaluate whether bounded multimodal gain can be recovered using only legal metadata available at query time, and show that direction-specific thresholding and regression-based gain prediction provide statistically supported but modest improvements.
    \item \textbf{Score-aware expert combination under non-answer-aware constraints.} We show that fixed-expert score patterns expose substantially stronger deployable signal than strict query-level metadata. Learned candidate-level routing improves over strict clean routing, while simple score interpolation further indicates that the main bottleneck lies in the information boundary rather than router complexity alone.
\end{enumerate}

These contributions do not imply that deployable routing fully closes every diagnostic gap. Strict clean routing recovers part of the bounded multimodal gain, score-aware expert combination recovers substantially more, and answer-aware Oracle analyses remain diagnostic references rather than deployable evidence. Since score interpolation can create rankings not identical to either fixed expert, the hard-selection Oracle should not be interpreted as an upper bound for score-level combination.

\section{Related Work}
\subsection{MMKGC and Multimodal Fusion}
Classical knowledge graph completion (KGC) methods, including translational, bilinear, tensor-factorization, convolutional, and graph-based models, provide the structural baselines for link prediction~\cite{nickel2011rescal,bordes2013transe,yang2015distmult,trouillon2016complex,dettmers2018conve,sun2019rotate,balazevic2019tucker,schlichtkrull2018rgcn,vashishth2020compgcn}. These models establish the central reference point for evaluating whether auxiliary modalities add value beyond graph structure. As multimodal knowledge graphs have become more common, MMKGC research has extended this structural paradigm by incorporating textual descriptions, visual features, and other modality-specific signals into entity representations. Early image-aware and multimodal embedding methods, together with benchmark-oriented work such as the \texttt{MMKG} line, share the basic premise that non-structural evidence can complement graph topology when structure alone is insufficient~\cite{xie2017ikrl,mousselly2018multimodal,pezeshkpour2018mkbe,liu2019mmkg}.

Most MMKGC methods therefore focus on \emph{how to fuse} heterogeneous information. Simple early-fusion strategies concatenate or aggregate modality-specific features before scoring, while more advanced methods use learned gates, attention, ensemble mechanisms, relation-aware transformations, or cross-modal interaction to adapt the contribution of each modality~\cite{ngiam2011multimodal,atrey2010multimodalfusion,arevalo2017gmu,zhao2022mose,liang2023hrgat,shang2024lafa,chen2022hybrid,jian2025amit,zhu2025modalities,gao2025mixed}. The common intuition behind these methods is that multimodal usefulness is context-dependent: the same image or text description may be informative for one relation but much less useful for another.

Our work is related to this fusion-oriented literature, and our \texttt{Gate-only} and \texttt{Full Model} variants naturally sit within this tradition. However, the present paper is not primarily a stronger-fusion paper. Prior MMKGC work mainly asks how multimodal evidence should be fused once it is available. By contrast, our starting point is that under the current OpenBG-IMG protocol, multimodal gain is not globally reliable. We therefore shift the emphasis from \emph{how to fuse} toward \emph{when to activate} multimodal evidence under protocol-shaped missing visual support.

\subsection{Missing Modality, Modality Imbalance, and Robustness}
A second strand of related work concerns robustness under incomplete, imbalanced, or unreliable modalities. In many real-world multimodal settings, entities do not have uniformly available text or images, and even when multimodal features exist, their quality may vary substantially. This has motivated broader work on incomplete multimodal learning, modality imbalance, robustness to noisy side information, and the question of whether visual context is always helpful for knowledge-rich prediction problems~\cite{atrey2010multimodalfusion,ramachandram2017deep,baltrusaitis2019multimodal,wang2021rsme}. Within MMKGC specifically, existing work already suggests that multimodal gains should not be assumed to appear uniformly or to depend only on stronger fusion operators~\cite{wang2021rsme,xu2022relation}.

This perspective is especially important for product-oriented knowledge graphs. In such settings, image availability may be highly uneven across entity subsets, relation types, or prediction directions. Under the current OpenBG-IMG protocol, the issue is even sharper: missingness is not only present, but protocol-shaped, because target position is already entangled with modality availability. As a result, missing-modality effects are not merely a nuisance variable; they help determine which modeling path becomes dominant under evaluation.

Our work is aligned with this robustness-oriented perspective, but differs in emphasis. Existing missing-modality work often focuses on making multimodal models more robust when some modalities are absent or noisy. We instead study a protocol-defined missingness pattern and ask where multimodal gain actually appears under that pattern. The resulting problem is not only robustness to missing images, but also clean decision-making under role--modality asymmetry.

\subsection{Selective Activation, Expert Routing, and Score-aware Model Combination}
A third strand of related work comes from selective computation, expert routing, and mixture-of-experts reasoning. Across machine learning settings, routing mechanisms are used when different experts are expected to perform well under different input conditions. Classical mixtures of experts provide the conceptual origin of this perspective, while later conditional-computation systems show how expert routing can become a general design principle for activating only the most suitable path~\cite{jacobs1991adaptive,jordan1994hierarchical,shazeer2017outrageously,lepikhin2021gshard,fedus2022switch}.

This routing view is relevant once multimodal gain is understood as conditional rather than globally reliable. If multimodal evidence helps only in certain protocol-shaped regions of the evaluation space, then always-on fusion becomes only one possible design choice. An alternative is to treat multimodal activation itself as a decision problem: use the multimodal path when the current query is likely to benefit from it, and fall back to a stronger structural path otherwise.

Unlike large-scale mixture-of-experts systems that route among many experts for model scaling, our routing problem is a clean query-level selection problem between a fusion expert and a structural expert under strict deployability constraints. The router may use only query-time legal signals, such as direction, relation-derived development priors, and observed-side modality indicators. It may not use the hidden target, target-side image availability, or answer-aware expert scores.

The new score-aware expert-combination line adds a second deployable but less restrictive setting. It is related to score-based ensembling, confidence-aware model combination, and learning-to-rank reranking with multiple scorers, because the combiner can observe expert score patterns for candidate entities after the fixed experts have been evaluated. Unlike generic ensemble or MoE systems, however, our score-aware line is motivated by a protocol-aware bounded-gain diagnosis and is evaluated under an explicit separation between strict metadata-only clean routing, score-aware non-answer-aware combination, and Oracle answer-aware routing.

Strong structural baselines are therefore not incidental comparison points in our setting, but part of the explanation itself. Under the current OpenBG-IMG protocol, \texttt{ComplEx} remains a competitive structural anchor and \texttt{Residual-only} is the strongest model within our internal family, whereas \texttt{TuckER} does not emerge as competitive under the present split. This matters because the key empirical tension in our paper is not between multimodal models and weak references, but between local multimodal gains and structure-dominant alternatives at the aggregate level.

Against this background, the present work is an analysis-driven method paper rather than a new-fusion or universal missing-modality paper. We begin from a protocol-aware diagnosis that multimodal gain is real but bounded under the current OpenBG-IMG setting, then extend this diagnosis into selective activation under explicit information boundaries.

\section{Problem Formulation and Methodology}
\subsection{Problem Definition and OpenBG-IMG Protocol}
We study multimodal knowledge graph completion on OpenBG-IMG under standard filtered link prediction. For a triple $(h, r, t)$, the task is to rank candidate tails for $(h, r, ?)$ and candidate heads for $(?, r, t)$. Because entities may have text and image information but modality availability is uneven, the setting is not only about multimodal representation learning; it is also about how the evaluation protocol shapes where multimodal evidence can matter.

All experiments use the current OpenBG-IMG \texttt{paper\_split}, which is the unified split employed for official model comparison, subgroup analysis, relation-group analysis, and routing evaluation. OpenBG-IMG is derived from the OpenBG business knowledge graph benchmark introduced by Deng et al.~\cite{deng2022openbg}.

\subsection{Protocol-Aware Bounded-Gain Diagnosis}
The protocol asymmetry above is the basis for our bounded-gain diagnosis. Under the current OpenBG-IMG split, target-side image availability differs by prediction role, so multimodal evidence is not available under a symmetric bidirectional regime. This means that always-on fusion may help in local image-supported regions while still harming or diluting performance in the globally dominant structure-favorable region. The experiments in \Cref{tab:official_model_comparison,tab:subgroup_results} quantify this pattern; here we use it to motivate selective activation.

\subsection{Strict Query-Level Clean Routing}
The previous sections establish a protocol-aware empirical tension. Under the current OpenBG-IMG setting, multimodal gain is visible but not globally reliable. \texttt{Full Model} improves over weaker multimodal baselines, yet stronger structural alternatives---especially \texttt{Residual-only}, and in the official model-comparison line also \texttt{ComplEx}---remain superior at the global level. This shifts the central problem away from merely constructing a richer multimodal encoder. Instead, the problem becomes whether multimodal fusion should be activated for the current query at all, and if so, how that decision should be made under clean query-time legality.

We therefore move from always-on multimodal fusion to \textbf{clean query-level selective activation}. The central idea is simple: if multimodal gain is conditional rather than uniformly available, then the system should predict when fusion is likely to help and use a stronger structural fallback otherwise. To operationalize this idea, we study query-level expert selection between a fusion expert and a structural expert.

We define two fixed experts:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Fusion expert:} \texttt{Gate-only}
    \item \textbf{Structural expert:} \texttt{Residual-only}
\end{itemize}

This choice is deliberate: \texttt{Gate-only} represents relation-aware multimodal fusion, while \texttt{Residual-only} represents structure-heavy compensation within the same internal model family.

\paragraph{Clean legality constraint.} For the strict query-level clean line, the key methodological boundary is clean query-time legality. Strict clean routing may use only information that is available before candidate scoring, including query direction, relation identity and development-derived relation priors, and query-observable modality indicators from the observed side of the query. By contrast, the deployable strict clean setting may not use hidden target-side information, target-side image labels, target-aware regimes, correct-target scores, or any other signals that require the true missing entity or post-hoc expert outcomes at inference time.

\paragraph{Naive global-threshold clean routing.} The simplest clean routing formulation uses a learned probability $p(q)$ and a single global threshold $\tau$. Let $x_q$ denote a clean feature vector available at query time. The router predicts
\[
p(q)=P(y=1\mid x_q),
\]
where $y=1$ means that the fusion expert should be selected. At inference time, we define
\[
\alpha(q)=\mathbf{1}[p(q)>\tau],
\]
and the final score becomes
\[
s_{\text{final}}(q,e)=\alpha(q)s_f(q,e)+(1-\alpha(q))s_s(q,e).
\]
This formulation serves as a minimal deployable clean baseline rather than the strongest routing policy considered in the paper.

\paragraph{Structured clean threshold policies.} The new results of this paper show that the weakness of the naive clean router is not solely due to signal scarcity. It is also caused by policy granularity. Under the present protocol, head-side and tail-side queries operate under different gain regimes, so a single global threshold is too rigid. We therefore study a stronger family of \textbf{structured clean threshold policies}. The most important member is \textbf{direction-specific thresholding}, which replaces the single global threshold with two query-direction-dependent thresholds:
\[
\alpha(q)=
\begin{cases}
\mathbf{1}[p(q)>\tau_{head}], & q \text{ is a head-prediction query}, \\
\mathbf{1}[p(q)>\tau_{tail}], & q \text{ is a tail-prediction query}.
\end{cases}
\]
Optional bucketized thresholding variants are treated only as supporting structured-policy extensions.

\paragraph{Target-aligned supervision for clean routing.} The second major refinement concerns the training target. In the original gain-threshold formulation, supervision is derived from a coarse binary gain label. For each query $q$, let $RR_f(q)$ and $RR_s(q)$ denote the reciprocal ranks of the correct target under the fusion and structural experts. The gain difference is
\[
\Delta(q)=RR_f(q)-RR_s(q).
\]
The original binary label is then defined as
\[
y(q)=\mathbf{1}[\Delta(q)>\delta].
\]
This label is deployable as a training target because it is constructed from development-side expert outcomes, but it is coarse: it discards the magnitude of the advantage or disadvantage of fusion.

To better align supervision with the actual expert difference, we study stronger clean targets. The regression formulation trains a model to predict a scalar gain estimate
\[
\widehat{\Delta}(q) \approx \Delta(q),
\]
using the same clean query-time feature vector $x_q$. At inference time, the regression router converts this estimate into a routing decision through a development-selected threshold $\theta$:
\[
\alpha_{reg}(q)=\mathbf{1}[\widehat{\Delta}(q)>\theta].
\]
The final score is then computed with the same expert-selection rule as before,
\[
s_{\text{final}}(q,e)=\alpha_{reg}(q)s_f(q,e)+(1-\alpha_{reg}(q))s_s(q,e).
\]
Thus, E5 differs from the naive binary clean router not by using post-hoc test-time information, but by preserving gain magnitude during training. Ordinal gain buckets are evaluated only as supporting ablations.

\paragraph{Clean router features and training.}
The deployable clean line uses only legal query-time features: direction, relation identity, development-derived relation priors, and observed-side modality indicators. It does not use hidden targets, target-aware subgroup labels, answer-aware confidence signals, or test-side expert outcomes during training. We compare a conservative clean rule, naive global-threshold routing, and stronger clean formulations based on structured thresholds and target-aligned supervision.

Taken together, these design choices reformulate MMKGC under the current OpenBG-IMG protocol from always-on multimodal fusion to strict query-level selective activation. The paper therefore studies not only a naive global-threshold clean baseline, but also stronger structured clean policies and more target-aligned clean supervision. In the results, E5 is treated as the strongest strict metadata-only clean router, not as the final upper limit of deployable selective activation.

\subsection{Score-aware Expert Combination}
Strict query-level clean routing deliberately excludes all candidate scores. This keeps the setting maximally clean, but it also makes the router coarse: it must decide whether fusion helps before seeing how either expert scores the candidate set. We therefore add a second deployable but less restrictive line: score-aware non-answer-aware expert combination. In this line, both fixed experts are first evaluated over candidate entities, and the combination policy may use their score patterns without using the hidden target, target image availability, reciprocal rank, or test labels during router training.

This line includes two families. The first family consists of simple score-interpolation baselines that combine the fixed Gate-only and Residual-only scores with global, direction-specific, or relation-specific weights selected on the development split. The second family consists of learned candidate-level score-aware routers, CA-S2 and CA-S3, which predict candidate-specific fusion weights from non-answer-aware score, rank, confidence, and disagreement features. This design allows us to test whether the main gain comes from learning a more complex router or from exposing fixed-expert score information at all.

For simple score interpolation, the mixed score is
\[
s_{\text{mix}}(q,e)=\alpha(q)s_f(q,e)+(1-\alpha(q))s_s(q,e),
\]
where $s_f$ and $s_s$ denote the fixed fusion and structural expert scores. In the global variant, $\alpha(q)=\alpha$ is a single scalar selected on the development split. In the direction-specific variant, $\alpha(q)=\alpha_{\text{head}}$ for head prediction and $\alpha(q)=\alpha_{\text{tail}}$ for tail prediction. In the relation-specific variant, $\alpha(q)=\alpha_r$ is selected per relation when development support is sufficient, with low-support relations falling back to the global weight.

For the learned candidate-level router, the fusion weight becomes candidate-specific:
\[
\alpha(q,e)=\sigma(g_\theta(x_q,x_e,z_{q,e})).
\]
Here $x_q$ contains legal query-level features, $x_e$ contains candidate metadata features, and $z_{q,e}$ contains non-answer-aware score-aware features derived from the fixed experts, such as score values, ranks, confidence statistics, and expert disagreement. The final mixed score is
\[
s_{\text{mix}}(q,e)=\alpha(q,e)s_f(q,e)+(1-\alpha(q,e))s_s(q,e).
\]

We evaluate three candidate-level variants. CA-S1 uses clean candidate metadata features and tests whether static candidate modality information is sufficient. CA-S2 uses score-aware expert features and tests whether deployable score, rank, confidence, and disagreement signals expose useful routing structure. CA-S3 combines clean candidate metadata with score-aware features. CA-S2 and CA-S3 are not strict metadata-only clean routers. They are deployable non-answer-aware routers: they require running both fixed experts and computing candidate scores before routing, but they do not use the hidden target, reciprocal rank, target image label, target-aware regime label, or answer-aware expert outcomes as features.

Candidate routers are trained with a ranking-aligned pairwise objective. For a positive target candidate $e^+$ and a sampled negative candidate $e^-$, the pairwise loss is
\[
\mathcal{L}_{pair}=-\log \sigma(s_{\text{mix}}(q,e^+)-s_{\text{mix}}(q,e^-)).
\]
Training uses hard candidate sets derived from the fixed experts for efficiency, including high-ranked negatives from the fusion and structural expert lists. Final CA-S1/CA-S2/CA-S3 reporting, however, uses full filtered ranking over all candidate entities rather than top-$K$ reranking, so the reported candidate-router results remain comparable as full-ranking test metrics.

\subsection{Information Boundaries and Training Protocol}

\paragraph{Clean router supervision.}
Router training uses development-side query-level feature tables constructed from the fixed fusion and structural experts. For every development query $q$, we record the filtered reciprocal ranks produced by the fusion expert and the structural expert, and compute
\[
\Delta(q)=RR_f(q)-RR_s(q).
\]
This development-side expert difference is used only to construct supervision targets, not as a test-time feature. Clean query features include direction, relation-level priors estimated from development outcomes, and modality indicators from the observed side of the query. For tail prediction $(h,r,?)$, the observed side is $h$; for head prediction $(?,r,t)$, the observed side is $t$. Strict clean routing never uses the hidden target entity, target-side image availability, target-aware subgroup labels, test labels, or answer-aware expert scores.

For binary clean routing, the label is
\[
y(q)=\mathbf{1}[\Delta(q)>\delta].
\]
For regression-based routing, the target is the scalar gain $\Delta(q)$ itself. At inference time, the regression score is converted into a hard routing decision through a development-selected threshold $\theta$:
\[
\alpha_{reg}(q)=\mathbf{1}[\widehat{\Delta}(q)>\theta].
\]

At test time, strict clean routing constructs $x_q$ from legal query-time features, predicts either a fusion probability or expected gain, applies the development-selected policy threshold, and then ranks candidates with the selected expert score. This procedure is intentionally simple because the scientific question is how much bounded gain can be recovered from metadata-only observability, not whether a complex router can be engineered under hidden target access.

\paragraph{Model selection and significance.}
All routing hyperparameters are selected on the development split only, including gain margins, thresholds, and interpolation weights. Once these choices are fixed, the selected policy is applied once to the test split for final reporting. The main significance evidence uses paired query-level bootstrap over matched reciprocal-rank differences, so confidence intervals compare policies on the same query instances rather than as unrelated aggregate scores.

\section{Experiments}
\subsection{Experimental Setup}
All experiments are conducted on the current OpenBG-IMG \texttt{paper\_split} under the unified protocol defined in the previous section. Base-model checkpoints are selected on the development split, and final base-model metrics are reported on the test split using filtered ranking with \texttt{direction=both}. Unless otherwise stated, official main-result metrics are aggregated over three random seeds and reported as mean $\pm$ standard deviation when the source line supports that form.

We also report an appendix-only robustness check on a relation-stratified and coverage-protected split, denoted \texttt{appendix\_split\_seed20260427}. For routing comparisons, paired bootstrap evidence is computed over matched query-level reciprocal-rank differences. Score-interpolation baselines are deterministic full-ranking combinations of fixed expert scores selected on the development split.

This section is organized around three research questions. \textbf{RQ1} asks whether multimodal gain is globally reliable under the current protocol. \textbf{RQ2} asks how much bounded gain strict metadata-only routing can recover once clean policy structure and supervision form are improved. \textbf{RQ3} asks whether score-aware non-answer-aware expert combination recovers substantially more deployable gain.

To answer these questions cleanly, we distinguish among four complementary evaluation lines. The \textbf{official model-comparison line} is used for the seven-model comparison among \texttt{Text-only}, \texttt{Early Fusion}, \texttt{Gate-only}, \texttt{Residual-only}, \texttt{Full Model}, \texttt{ComplEx}, and \texttt{TuckER}. The \textbf{strict query-level clean routing line} is based on query-level exported expert predictions and unified recomputation under legal metadata-only query-time constraints. The \textbf{score-aware expert-combination line} is based on fixed-expert score exports and full filtered-ranking recomputation for score interpolation and CA-S1/CA-S2/CA-S3. The \textbf{Oracle/post-hoc analysis line} is retained only for stronger offline separability analysis and hard-selection upper-bound-style comparisons; it is not part of the deployable claim.

For readability, we refer to the best direction-specific clean thresholding policy as \textbf{E1} and the best regression-based clean router as \textbf{E5} in the following discussion. E1 represents the strongest structured-threshold clean policy, whereas E5 represents the strongest target-aligned clean-supervision policy.

\subsection{RQ1: Is multimodal gain globally reliable?}
RQ1 asks whether multimodal gain is globally reliable under the current OpenBG-IMG protocol. Our starting point is that the benchmark is not a neutral multimodal setting. \subsection{RQ2: How much bounded gain can strict metadata-only routing recover?}
RQ2 asks how much bounded multimodal gain can be recovered under strict metadata-only query-level routing. \Cref{tab:main_clean_routing} shows that a naive learned router with one global threshold is too weak: it reaches 0.2939 MRR, below the clean rule baseline at 0.2943 MRR. Once the policy uses direction-specific thresholds, performance rises to 0.2974 MRR, with a strictly positive paired-bootstrap confidence interval against the clean rule. This isolates the first clean-routing bottleneck: the decision boundary must respect the role-asymmetric protocol rather than using one global operating point.

The next bottleneck is supervision form. Replacing the binary gain label with scalar gain prediction further improves the clean result to 0.2982 MRR. Ordinal gain modeling and delta-sensitivity results, reported in Appendix~\ref{app:delta_sensitivity}, support the same interpretation: preserving gain magnitude information is helpful under this shallow and asymmetric gain boundary. Despite these improvements, the best strict clean strategy remains far below hard-selection Oracle routing at 0.3337 MRR, leaving about 0.0356 MRR of unresolved hard-selection headroom.

The RQ2 conclusion is therefore deliberately bounded. Structured clean routing and more target-aligned clean supervision recover reliable but modest gains under strict metadata-only query-level constraints. E5 is the strongest strict clean result, but the remaining gap to hard-selection Oracle routing indicates that query-level metadata alone still hides much of the hard-selection expert signal. Additional visualization of this gap is provided in Appendix~\ref{app:clean_oracle_gap}.

\subsection{Integrated Discussion}
The combined evidence across RQ1--RQ3 supports a single conclusion. Under the current OpenBG-IMG protocol, multimodal gain is real but bounded, and strict metadata-only query-level routing can recover only a modest part of it. The clean-routing line identifies two local bottlenecks: a single global threshold is too coarse, and binary gain supervision discards useful gain-magnitude information. Direction-specific thresholding and regression-based routing address these issues, but they do not expose candidate-level expert behavior.

The score-aware line changes that boundary. CA-S1 shows that static candidate metadata alone is insufficient, whereas CA-S2 and CA-S3 show that learned score-aware candidate-level routing is much stronger than strict clean routing. The full-ranking interpolation results sharpen the conclusion further: simple global, direction-specific, and relation-specific score interpolation outperform CA-S2, indicating that the dominant source of recoverable gain is the availability of fixed-expert score-combination information rather than learned-router complexity alone.

The key finding is therefore not that multimodal evidence helps whenever target-side images exist. Image-supported head-side targets reveal relative fusion preference, but the strongest deployable improvement appears once non-answer-aware fixed-expert score patterns are available. In incomplete or asymmetric visual-support settings, deciding what deployable expert-confidence information to expose may be as important as designing a richer fusion operator.

\subsection{Limitations}

First, the conclusions are protocol-aware rather than universal. The role--modality asymmetry analyzed in this paper is induced by the current OpenBG-IMG \texttt{paper\_split}; different splits or datasets with more balanced target-side image availability may produce different gain boundaries. The thresholds, gain margins, and supervision targets should therefore be interpreted as empirical operating choices for this split rather than as transfer rules for other benchmarks or missing-modality patterns.

The additional appendix split provides a robustness check for the same qualitative pattern, but it does not make the conclusions dataset-universal: it preserves the role--modality asymmetric structure and confirms the same routing trend, while still remaining within the OpenBG-IMG setting.

Second, the strict query-level clean router is intentionally restricted to legal metadata-only query-time features. This makes the clean line maximally deployable, but it also limits separability. The gap between E5 and the score-aware expert-combination results indicates that useful expert-combination information is not visible from query-level metadata alone, even after policy structure and supervision form are improved.

Third, score-aware expert combination has a higher deployability cost than strict query-level clean routing. CA-S2 and CA-S3 require running both fixed experts and computing candidate-level score features before routing. Score interpolation also requires both fixed expert score vectors before combination. These methods are non-answer-aware and deployable after expert scoring, but they are not strict metadata-only clean routers.

Fourth, the full-ranking score-interpolation baselines show that CA-S2 is not the uniquely strongest score-aware combiner. This is a clarifying limitation of the learned-router component rather than a contradiction of the main analysis. The result indicates that once fixed-expert score information is available, simple interpolation can recover more gain than the learned candidate-level router. Therefore, the strongest claim of the paper is about the score-aware information boundary and expert-combination basis, not about the architectural superiority of CA-S2.

Fifth, the routing framework uses a fixed pair of experts, \texttt{Gate-only} and \texttt{Residual-only}. This design makes the analysis interpretable and keeps the comparison controlled, but it does not exhaust the possible expert set. Future work could explore additional multimodal and structural experts, stronger candidate-level feature families, or end-to-end expert-router training.

Finally, the main routing conclusions are based primarily on MRR-based full-ranking comparisons and, where applicable, paired bootstrap delta-MRR. Hit-based routing exports and oracle-gap decompositions are treated as auxiliary rather than primary evidence.

\section{Conclusion}
This paper addresses a protocol-aware question on OpenBG-IMG: not whether multimodal information is useful in the abstract, but whether it yields reliable gain under the current asymmetric missing-visual setting. We show that multimodal gain is real but local and bounded. The clearest relative fusion preference appears in \texttt{head\_has\_img}, whereas the globally dominant \texttt{tail\_no\_img} regime remains structure-favorable.

Strict metadata-only query-level routing recovers only modest gain. Direction-specific thresholding and regression-based gain prediction improve over naive clean routing, but query-level metadata still hides much of the recoverable expert signal. Once non-answer-aware fixed-expert score patterns are exposed, deployable expert combination becomes much stronger: CA-S2 reaches 0.3142 MRR, and simple full-ranking score interpolation reaches up to 0.3428 MRR.

The final message is therefore focused. Under the current OpenBG-IMG protocol, the main bottleneck is not merely router complexity, but the deployable information boundary under which expert combination is performed. Future MMKGC work under incomplete visual support should study not only how to fuse modalities more richly, but also how to structure simple, stable, and deployable expert-combination rules under explicit information boundaries.
