# Related Work

## 1. MMKGC and Multimodal Fusion

Research on knowledge graph completion (KGC) has long focused on learning from graph structure alone, with translational, bilinear, convolutional, and graph-based baselines such as `TransE`, `TransH`, `DistMult`, `ComplEx`, `ConvE`, `RotatE`, `TuckER`, `R-GCN`, and `CompGCN` forming the broader methodological background of modern link prediction. The classic review of relational machine learning for knowledge graphs also makes clear that strong structural modeling remains the core reference point for any completion study. As multimodal knowledge graphs have become more common, a growing body of work has extended this paradigm by incorporating textual descriptions, visual features, and other modality-specific signals into entity representations. Representative early MMKGC directions include image-aware knowledge representation learning (`IKRL`), multimodal translation-based representation learning, multimodal relational embedding, and the `MMKG` benchmark line, all of which share the premise that non-structural evidence can complement graph topology and improve completion when graph structure alone is insufficient.

Within this broader trend, one major line of work has focused on how to combine structural and non-structural representations more effectively. Simple early-fusion strategies concatenate or aggregate modality-specific features before scoring, while more advanced approaches use learned gates, attention, ensemble mechanisms, or relation-aware transformations to adapt the contribution of each modality. This design philosophy underlies methods such as `MoSE`, `HRGAT`, `LAFA`, `Hybrid Transformer`, `Adaptive Modality Interaction Transformer`, `Moodle`, and `Mixed-Curvature MMKGC`, as well as more general surveys of multimodal knowledge graphs and multimodal machine learning. The common intuition is that multimodal usefulness is context-dependent: the same image or text description may be highly informative for one relation but nearly irrelevant for another.

Our work is clearly related to this fusion-oriented literature, and our `Gate-only` and `Full Model` variants sit naturally within it. However, the present paper is not primarily a stronger-fusion paper. Most prior MMKGC work asks how multimodal evidence should be fused more effectively once it is available. By contrast, our starting point is that under the current OpenBG-IMG protocol, multimodal gain is not globally reliable. We therefore shift the emphasis from fusion design alone to a more diagnostic question: under what protocol-shaped conditions is multimodal evidence worth activating at all?

## 2. Missing Modality, Modality Imbalance, and Robustness

A second strand of related work concerns robustness under incomplete, imbalanced, or unreliable modalities. In many real-world multimodal settings, entities do not have uniformly available text or images, and even when multimodal features exist, their quality may vary substantially. This has motivated broader work on incomplete multimodal learning, modality imbalance, robustness to noisy side information, and the question of whether visual context is always helpful for knowledge-rich prediction problems. Within MMKGC specifically, work such as `Is Visual Context Really Helpful for Knowledge Graph?` and `Relation-enhanced Negative Sampling for Multimodal Knowledge Graph Completion` already suggests that multimodal gains should not be assumed to appear uniformly or to depend only on stronger fusion operators.

This perspective is especially important for product-oriented knowledge graphs. In such settings, image availability may be highly uneven across entity subsets, relation types, or prediction directions. Under the current OpenBG-IMG protocol, the issue is even sharper: missingness is not only present, but protocol-shaped, because target position is already entangled with modality availability. As a result, missing-modality effects are not merely a nuisance variable; they help determine which modeling path becomes dominant under evaluation.

Our work is aligned with this robustness-oriented perspective, but differs in emphasis. We do not propose a new generic missing-modality training algorithm. Instead, we use the current OpenBG-IMG protocol as a diagnostic setting in which incomplete visual support is already structured by the benchmark itself. This allows us to ask a more precise question than standard robustness work: where multimodal gain actually appears under this protocol, and under what query conditions that gain should be trusted.

## 3. Selective Activation, Routing, and Structural Baselines

A third strand of related work comes from selective computation, expert routing, and mixture-of-experts reasoning. Across machine learning settings, routing mechanisms are used when different experts are expected to perform well under different input conditions. Rather than forcing one path to handle every instance equally, a router predicts which expert should be trusted for the current input, or whether a more expensive branch is worth activating at all. Classical mixtures of experts provide the conceptual origin of this perspective, while later conditional-computation systems such as sparsely gated MoE, `GShard`, and `Switch Transformers` show how expert routing can become a central systems-level design principle rather than only a small modeling trick.

This routing view is highly relevant once multimodal gain is understood as conditional rather than globally reliable. If multimodal evidence helps only in certain protocol-shaped regions of the evaluation space, then always-on fusion becomes only one possible design choice. An alternative is to treat multimodal activation itself as a decision problem: use the multimodal path when the current query is likely to benefit from it, and fall back to a stronger structural path otherwise. In this sense, the present paper is related not only to adaptive multimodal fusion, but also to instance-level expert selection.

Strong structural baselines are therefore not incidental comparison points in our setting, but part of the explanation itself. Under the current OpenBG-IMG protocol, `ComplEx` remains a competitive structural anchor and `Residual-only` is the strongest model within our internal family, whereas `TuckER` does not emerge as competitive under the present split. This matters because the key empirical tension in our paper is not between multimodal models and weak references, but between local multimodal gains and globally stronger structure-dominant alternatives. The broader KGC literature on translational, bilinear, and graph-based models is therefore not just historical background here; it provides the baseline family against which any claim of multimodal usefulness has to be interpreted.

Against this background, the present work should be understood as an analysis-driven method paper. It is not primarily a new-fusion paper, because our main claim is not that a proposed multimodal architecture is globally strongest. It is not primarily a generic missing-modality method paper, because our goal is not to introduce a universal robustness algorithm. Instead, we begin from a protocol-aware diagnosis: under the current OpenBG-IMG setting, multimodal gain is real but bounded, and its usefulness depends on target position, image availability, relation context, and branch competition. We then extend this diagnosis into a method contribution by reformulating bounded multimodal gain as a query-level selective-activation problem. The proposed gain-threshold router is therefore not a detached engineering add-on, but the operational response to the bounded-gain structure revealed by the current protocol.
